# Worked example: the packaging refresh

Fictional. Every name, business and number here is invented. Use it as the pattern to recognise, not as a script.

## The situation

Maya runs Northgate Studio, a two-person design studio. Three weeks ago she sent a proposal to Tom Reyes, head of marketing at Larkspur Homeware: a packaging refresh across 12 products for £14,000.

Tom replied the same day: "Love this. Taking it to finance." Then nothing for 12 days.

Today he writes:

> Hi Maya, sorry for the silence. Finance has come back at £7,000. Any chance you can make that work? We'd want to start next month, the new range launches in spring.

Maya pastes it and adds: "I want this one. Next month is half empty. But I'm not doing the whole thing for half."

## Step 1: Does she want it?

She wants it. She said so, and the half-empty month backs it up. So the job is the best terms, not a polite no.

## Step 2: The situation

- **The ask.** Win the work without teaching Larkspur that her price is half.
- **The other side.** Tom is the champion. Finance signs. Tom has already sold the work internally once.
- **The clock.** Theirs. The spring launch is a fixed date and Tom raised it himself.
- **Her alternative.** Weak. One other enquiry, nothing signed, half a month free.
- **Their alternative.** Moderate. They could find another studio, but that means a new brief, a new proposal and a new internal sell, against a spring deadline.
- **Already spent.** Tom's internal sell, three weeks, and his own enthusiasm on record.
- **Who did well from the old arrangement.** `[CHECK: does Larkspur have an in-house designer who did the current packaging?]`
- **Whose advice costs them nothing.** None in play. Tom is in-house and takes no cut.

## Step 3: The power map

|                    | Maya | Tom (and finance) |
|--------------------|------|-------------------|
| Wants              | The work, at a price that holds | The refresh before the spring launch, inside budget |
| Fears losing       | A half-empty month | Missing the launch, and looking bad after championing it |
| Real alternative   | Weak: one unsigned enquiry | Moderate: start again with another studio |
| Clock pressure     | Low to medium | High: a fixed launch date |
| Who they answer to | Herself | Finance |

The "fears losing" row decides it. Tom has more at risk than Maya does: a missed launch after he championed the proposal.

## Step 4: Fox or lion

Fox first, lion once. Her alternative is weak, so she cannot win a straight push on price. But the clock is theirs, so she can hold the headline price as the one point she will not move on.

## Step 5: The plays

1. **Never answer a stated number with one figure alone.** Hold £14,000 for the full refresh, and put a real option beside it at £7,000. Cost: she may end up doing the smaller job.
2. **Concede loudly on what costs nothing.** She can start in two weeks, and she can split payment across two budgets. Both cost her little and both look valuable to someone facing a launch date. Cost: a slower second invoice.
3. **Measure the ground.** Ask which option suits finance better. That tells her whether £7,000 is a cap for this year or a cap for the whole project. Cost: one more round of email.

## Step 6: The message

```
Hi Tom,

Thanks for coming back with a number, that really helps.

£7,000 doesn't stretch to all 12 products. The full refresh stays at £14,000.

Two ways it does work at £7,000:

1. The hero pack plus the design system, so your team can roll it across the rest.
2. The full refresh, split £7,000 now and £7,000 in the new financial year.

Either way I can start in two weeks, so you'd have finals well before the launch.

Which suits finance better?

Maya
```

The headline price is untouched. There is something real Tom can buy today at his number. And it ends on a question he can answer, not on a figure he has to reject.

## The move to avoid

Replying "Our price is £14,000" and nothing else. It does not read as firm. It reads as a decline, and Tom has no option to take back to finance, so the thread closes.

The second trap is quietly agreeing to the full refresh for £7,000 because the month is empty. It fills the month and sets her price at half for every future job with Larkspur.

The third is inventing urgency ("another client wants the slot"). The other enquiry is unsigned. If Tom calls the bluff, she loses the price and the trust together.
