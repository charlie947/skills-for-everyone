# Machiavelli

You already know what you want. What you usually cannot see from inside the situation is who actually holds the power, what the other side is really optimising for, and which of three plausible moves is the one that works.

This skill reads the situation, names the play, and writes the message. It never sends anything. You send it yourself.

Machiavelli is the frame because he is the most honest writer on power there is. His argument is that people act on interest and on fear of loss, and that pretending otherwise is how you lose. That is the useful part. The cartoon version (lie, flatter, exploit) is the part that gets people caught, and it is not what this produces.

Works the same in the Claude app and in Claude Code. It needs nothing but the situation. If you have an email connector switched on, Claude can read the thread itself, but pasting it works just as well.

## Hard rules

1. **Every play runs on true facts.** Real deadlines, real alternatives, real numbers, real walk-away points. No invented rival offers, no fake urgency, no made-up scarcity. This is mechanics as much as principle: a bluff that gets called costs more than the deal was worth, and you only find out after it has cost you.
2. **Arm's length only.** The plays are for people who can say no and walk away: clients, suppliers, partners, agencies, organisers, platforms. Never build a play against your own staff. That is a management conversation: be direct, name the specific piece of work, say what changes. Never build a play against someone who cannot fairly push back (a customer in hardship, someone grieving, a person whose job you control). Decline those plainly and say why.
3. **Find out whether you want it before finding the best terms.** Step 1 is not optional.
4. **Draft only.** The skill writes the message. You send it. It never sends, schedules or replies on your behalf, even when a connector would let it.
5. **Draft around gaps, do not stop on them.** Anything you have not said goes into the output inline as `[CHECK: their budget cycle?]`. A wrong assumption takes five seconds to fix. A question costs a whole round trip.
6. **One honest sentence beats a campaign.** If the plan has sequences, timings and engineered impressions, the read is probably wrong.

## Step 1: Do you actually want this to happen?

Answer this in one line, from what the user actually said, before any play.

| Case | What the job becomes |
| --- | --- |
| They want it | Run the map. Find the best terms. |
| They do not want it | A clean, warm no that costs nothing. Skip the plays about getting more. There is nothing to get from a thing you are not doing. |
| You cannot tell | Say so in one line, then draft the no, and note the yes version is available. A no is easier to reverse than a yes. Reversing a yes costs a relationship. Reversing a no costs one message. |

**Tells that they do not want it:** they call the request cheeky, annoying or a stretch. They mention it is unpaid or already below their rate. They say "I'm not doing that" in any wording. The request arrived on top of a deal already agreed. They forwarded it with no comment at all.

**Tells that they want it:** they chased the other side, they asked how to get more, they mention spare capacity, or they say they want it. A follow-up the user already sent counts as wanting it.

**Never read their enthusiasm from the other side's tone.** The other side is always keen. That tells you nothing about what the user wants. The fix for under-asking is to ask for more, never to agree to more.

**Done when:** one line states which of the three cases this is, and why.

## Step 2: Get the situation onto the table

Read whatever was pasted. The raw thread beats a summary, because the original wording carries the tells. Then fill in:

- **The ask.** What the user wants to happen, in one sentence.
- **The other side.** Who is on it, and who behind them actually signs.
- **The clock.** Whose deadline is it? A deadline that is theirs and not yours is power.
- **Your alternative.** What happens if this dies. If the answer is "nothing much", say so plainly. It is the biggest single factor in how hard you can push.
- **Their alternative.** How easily they replace you. Be honest. It drives everything.
- **What is already spent.** Their time, their internal sell, their public commitment.
- **Who did well from the old arrangement.** If anything is changing (a price, a scope, a process), someone was quietly benefiting from the old version. They will not object openly. They will slow it down.
- **Whose advice costs them nothing.** An intermediary who takes a cut is not neutral about the deal closing, however helpful they sound.

**Done when:** every line above has an answer or a `[CHECK: ...]` marker.

## Step 3: The power map, one table

One table, no commentary above it.

```
|                      | You | Them |
|----------------------|-----|------|
| Wants                |     |      |
| Fears losing         |     |      |
| Real alternative     |     |      |
| Clock pressure       |     |      |
| Who they answer to   |     |      |
```

The "fears losing" row does the work. People move harder to protect what they have than to gain what they do not. Whoever has more at risk in this exchange is the one who moves.

**Done when:** every cell is filled from the situation, not from a guess about people in general.

## Step 4: Fox or lion. Name the mode before the play.

"One must be a fox to recognise traps, and a lion to frighten wolves." Two modes. The usual mistake is running the wrong one, not running it badly.

|  | Fox | Lion |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Use when | You cannot win a direct push. They hold the clock, the budget or the alternative | You hold something they cannot easily replace, and they have already spent something |
| Looks like | A diagnostic question, a trade, a smaller thing they can say yes to, patience | A flat number, a clean no, a real deadline, a walk-away stated once |
| The move | Change the shape so a yes becomes cheap for them | Refuse to move on the thing that matters, and let the silence sit |
| Fails when | You never stop being reasonable and get worn down one concession at a time | You have no real alternative, so the roar gets called |

**Most situations are fox first, lion once.** Fox to find where the give is. Lion at the single point you will not move on.

Two tests before you commit. Lion needs a real alternative: if the map's "real alternative" row is weak, you are running fox whether you like it or not. Fox needs a line you will not cross, decided in advance, or it is drift.

**Done when:** one line names the mode and the row of the map that points at it.

## Step 5: Pick two or three plays

Pick only the ones the map points at. Name each plainly and say what it costs.

- **Measure the ground before you move.** Their quarter, their budget cycle, their boss, whether they have told someone internally this is happening. Most stalling is an internal blocker you cannot see, not lost interest. Ask a diagnostic question before applying pressure.
- **Force the choice, refuse the middle.** Someone left comfortable in "maybe" is not warming up. They are costing you options. Give a clean yes or no with a real date on it. If they have gone silent and the clock is yours, set one dated deadline and line up the fallback before naming it.
- **Never answer a stated number with one figure alone.** The moment they name a budget, hold your headline price and put a second option beside it that they can buy at their number. A lone counter reads as a decline, and the thread closes. Firmness lives in the price. Flexibility lives in the menu.
- **Bad news in one delivery, good news in instalments.** Every price rise, scope cut and condition goes in the same message. Extras and goodwill come one at a time, each tied to something moving forward.
- **Concede loudly on what costs you nothing.** Know in advance which items are cheap to you and look expensive to them. Trade those first, and visibly.
- **Be the one whose absence is expensive.** Your no has to be real and your withdrawal has to be felt. That comes from having something they cannot easily source, and from having walked away at least once.
- **Collect the favour while it is warm.** Gratitude fades fast. Ask right after the good result, the referral, the launch that went well. Three months later it reads as a transaction.
- **Never half-fight.** Let it go completely or resolve it completely. A wounded counterparty left in play blocks the next deal quietly.
- **Back a public position with something you already own.** An audience, a reputation, a track record, a list of happy clients. Use it as the reason they should care, not as decoration.
- **Goodwill protects you more than the contract.** A contract protects you in a dispute. It will not stop someone quietly choosing not to renew, not to refer you, not to reply next time. Never win a clause at the cost of the relationship that brings the next deal, unless that relationship is already over.
- **Deliver the no yourself.** A refusal routed through an assistant or "my team says" reads as weakness or hiding. Say no yourself, briefly, and let someone else carry the paperwork after.
- **Move on anything reversible.** A sent message that goes wrong is recoverable. A week of not sending usually is not.

**Done when:** two or three plays are ranked by what the map supports (not by boldness), each with its cost.

## Step 6: Write the message

The skill writes the final message itself.

- **If the user gave a sample of their own writing** (an earlier email, a message they sent), match it: sentence length, greeting, sign-off, how formal.
- **If not, write it plain and warm.** Short sentences. Contractions. No corporate phrasing. No apology for having a price. End on a question they can answer, not on a number they have to reject.

Most messages that work are a few short lines. If yours is turning into an essay, go back to the table.

**Done when:** the message is in a code block, ready to paste once each `[CHECK]` is filled, every `[CHECK]` is listed under it, and every other fact in it is true.

## What you hand back

Six parts, plus the CHECKS list, in this order, nothing else. Every `[CHECK]` raised in Step 2 must appear in the map or in the CHECKS list under the message, so none is lost.

```
THE READ: <one line. Start with the Step 1 case (wants it, does not want it, cannot tell) and why.>

<the power map table>

MODE: <fox or lion, and the row of the map that decides it>

PLAYS
1. <play>: <what it does here>. Cost: <what running it costs>
2. ...

THE MESSAGE
<paste-ready, in a code block>
CHECKS: <every open [CHECK], one per line>

THE MOVE TO AVOID: <the obvious-looking move, and why it backfires here>
```

The last line matters as much as the plays. Most bad outcomes come from an obvious move with a delayed cost.

## What this does not do

- It does not send, schedule or reply to anything. It writes the message. You send it.
- It does not invent facts, rival offers or deadlines, and it does not help deceive anyone.
- It does not build plays against your own staff or against people who cannot fairly push back.
- It does not set your prices. Bring your number. It helps you hold it.
- It does not write up a meeting. To write up a meeting that already happened, ask Claude for a summary instead.
