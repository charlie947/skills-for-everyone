## What it does

Machiavelli reads a real business situation for power, then writes the message. You paste the thread from a client who has gone quiet, a partner pushing terms or a buyer who has just named a budget. You get back who holds the power, what the other side is really optimising for, the play that fits, and a message ready to paste.

It asks whether you want the thing at all before it looks for the best terms. A keen counterparty tells you nothing about what you want, and a clever yes to something you never wanted is the most expensive output it could produce. Every play runs on true facts. It never invents a rival offer or a deadline, and it never sends anything. You send it.

## When to reach for it

Type `/machiavelli`, or Claude reaches for it when you paste a thread from a client, supplier, partner or organiser and ask what to do about it.

| Situation | Use |
| --- | --- |
| A deal has stalled, gone quiet, or come back with a lower number | `machiavelli` |
| You need to decide whether to push, trade or walk | `machiavelli` |
| A meeting just happened and you need the record, actions and recap | Neither. Ask Claude for a summary of the call |
| A hard conversation with someone on your own team | Neither. That is management, not a negotiation. Be direct and name the specific piece of work |

The fastest input is the raw thread plus one line on what you want to happen. Do not summarise it first. The original wording carries the tells.

## The power map and the two modes

Everything hangs on one table: what each side wants, fears losing, could do instead, how much time pressure they are under, and who they answer to. The "fears losing" row does the work. People move harder to protect what they have than to gain what they do not, so whoever has more at risk is the one who moves.

The map then decides the mode:

- **Fox** when you cannot win a direct push. Ask, trade, offer something smaller they can say yes to.
- **Lion** when you hold something they cannot easily replace. A flat number, a clean no, stated once.

Most situations are fox first, lion once: fox to find where the give is, lion at the one point you will not move on.

## Common questions

**It drafted a yes to something I did not want to do. Why?**
It read enthusiasm from the other side's tone. That is why the first step now asks whether you want this to happen at all. If it cannot tell, it drafts the no and offers the yes. A no costs one message to reverse. A yes costs a relationship.

**They named a budget and I countered with my price. They went silent. What went wrong?**
A single figure in reply to their number reads as a decline, so they close the thread instead of taking it back to their team. Hold your headline price and put a second option beside it they can buy at their number. Firmness lives in the price. Flexibility lives in the menu.

**The play was right but the message did not sound like me.**
Give it one or two messages you have written before. It matches your greeting, sentence length and sign-off. Without a sample it writes plain and warm, which is better than corporate but is still not you.

## It's working if

- The first line tells you whether you want to do this, before any tactic.
- The message is a few short lines and ends on a question, not on a number they have to reject.
- Every fact in the message is one you could defend if they asked.
- You get one "move to avoid" that you were, honestly, about to make.
